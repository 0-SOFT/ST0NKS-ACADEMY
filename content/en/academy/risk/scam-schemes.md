Real arbitrage has a clean reputation: buy low on one venue, sell high on another, little risk left over.
That reputation is exactly what makes "risk-free income" such a convenient bait — the phrase sounds
plausible because it almost fits the real thing. The difference is one detail, and it is worth learning
before any of the schemes.

## The one tell that gives all of them away

In a real arbitrage trade both legs open where your money **already sits**: you buy on one venue and
sell on another, sending nothing to anyone in advance. Every scam works the other way — **you transfer
funds first**, and the promised thing happens afterwards. Or it doesn't. The moment a step asks you to
send money to a stranger "to get started," you already have your answer.

## Six familiar baits

**Fake platforms and "bots."** A site shows a pretty cross-exchange spread and urges you to enter a
trade now. You top up the "exchange" or "bot" — no trade happens, support goes quiet, the site vanishes.
Check the domain, its age, a living community; never fund a place with nothing to verify.

**Someone else's seed phrase.** A comment "accidentally" leaves the key to a wallet holding five
thousand USDT — please help me withdraw. The wallet is real, the money is real, but it can't move: no
gas. You send a coin "for the fee," and it goes straight to the scammer. The trick relies on the victim
feeling clever rather than robbed. There is nothing to tell apart here: using a stranger's seed phrase
is never safe.

**Shared "pools" and managed funds.** Money collected for joint trades, screenshots of returns, and a
week later the channel is gone. The tell is a promised fixed percentage, especially "in any market" and
"no risk."

**Paid signals.** A private chat sells access to "super pairs." Inside: yesterday's screenshots and dead
pairs. A price tag from fifty to five hundred dollars guarantees nothing.

**Fake dual listing.** News that a coin is about to be added on two exchanges at once — hurry. You buy an
illiquid token on one venue while the second is a fake or "frozen," and there is no one to sell to.

**Empty DEX pair.** An illiquid coin is seeded into a pool to fake a large spread against an exchange.
You buy on the DEX hoping to sell higher — but the exchange doesn't list it, and the pool is drained.

## How not to get caught

- Don't believe "risk-free" 5–10% a day. Independent measurements put retail at roughly 4–20% **a year**
  (as of September 2026).
- Verify any promise across several sources.
- Don't send money to strangers or "bots."
- Never touch someone else's seed phrase.
- Use tools and communities you can actually check.

## The takeaway

Don't memorize six schemes — there will be a seventh. Keep one check instead: in real arbitrage you
**never send money ahead**. The moment that transfer appears in the plan, it isn't a pair, it's bait.
